**Quick answer:** Handwraps for boxing exist to compress the 27 small bones of
your hand into one solid unit and to support your wrist on impact. They are not
padding. Padding is what the glove is for. If you skip the wraps, every punch
you throw travels through loose, independently moving bones, and that is how
hands get hurt.

I have been wrapping hands at Ironhand Boxing since we opened in 2021, and it is
the first thing I teach a new boxer before they ever touch a bag. Below is what
handwraps actually do, how to put them on step by step, and how we wrap
differently on fight night.

## What are handwraps for in boxing?

Handwraps are a long strip of cotton or semi-elastic fabric, usually 180 inches,
that you wind around your wrist, palm, thumb, and knuckles before putting on
your gloves. Their job is structural, not protective in the cushioning sense.

Three things a handwrap does:

1. **It binds the metacarpals together.** Your hand has 27 bones. Punching with
   them loose is like swinging a bag of marbles. Wrapped, they act as a single
   mass.
2. **It braces the wrist.** Most beginner hand injuries are not broken knuckles,
   they are wrist sprains from a fist that folded on contact. The wrist anchor
   is the most important part of the wrap.
3. **It locks the thumb in.** A thumb that drifts away from the fist during a
   hook is a thumb that catches on an elbow or a headgear strap.

Here is the part most people get backwards: the wrap is not there to pad your
knuckles. The glove does that. The wrap tightens the hand into a solid fist so
the fingers and knuckles do not collapse into each other.

## Why do I need handwraps?

Because the alternative is a hand injury that takes you out of training for six
to twelve weeks and, in the case of a boxer's fracture, can leave a permanent
knuckle deformity.

You need handwraps if you are:

- **Hitting a heavy bag.** The bag does not move much. All that force comes back
  into your hand.
- **Doing mitt work.** Coaches move the pads. You will land off-center, and
  off-center is where wrists roll.
- **Sparring.** Even with 16 ounce gloves, sparring is the highest-volume impact
  work most amateurs do.
- **Renting gym gloves.** Wraps also absorb sweat and keep the inside of a glove
  from turning into a petri dish.

The only time you can reasonably skip them is shadowboxing and jump rope.
Everything else, wrap up. Our [group classes](/#schedule) all include bag work
and mitt work, so bring wraps to any of them.

A common objection I hear from new boxers is that wraps feel restrictive. That
is the point. If your hand can move freely inside the wrap, the wrap is not
doing its job.

## What handwraps should you buy?

For anyone training two to five days a week:

| Type | Length | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Standard cotton** | 180" | Most adults, all-purpose training. The default answer. |
| **Mexican style (semi-elastic)** | 180" | Slightly more stretch, conforms tighter, most popular in gyms. |
| **Cotton, shorter** | 120" | Smaller hands, teens, or wrapping under bag gloves with less room. |
| **Gel/quick wraps** | N/A | Convenience only. Fine for a class you are late to. Not a substitute for a real wrap on bag or sparring days. |

Buy at least three pairs so you always have a clean set. Wash them in a mesh
laundry bag on cold and hang dry. Heat kills the elastic.

If you are coming to your [first class](/new-clients/), do not buy anything yet.
We will wrap your hands for you and a coach will tell you what is actually worth
spending money on once you know you are staying.

## Fight night: gauze and tape

Everything above is a training wrap. Once you step into a sanctioned fight, the
rules change and so does the material.

> When we fight, we use gauze and tape.

Fight wraps are built from surgical gauze and athletic tape rather than a
reusable cloth wrap. Gauze conforms to the hand far more precisely, and tape
locks each layer down so nothing shifts across ten rounds. The result is a
harder, more custom-fitted structure than any cloth wrap can produce.

![An Ironhand fighter's completed gauze and tape wraps, palms up, before the gloves go on](/photos/fight-night-gauze-and-tape-hand-wrap.jpg)

The rules vary slightly by sanctioning body, but the general amateur and
professional standard limits the gauze to roughly 20 yards of two-inch soft
gauze per hand and the tape to about 10 feet of one-inch athletic tape per hand.
Tape cannot cover the knuckles. It runs between the fingers and anchors the
wrist. Both corners typically observe the wrapping, and an official initials the
finished wrap before the gloves go on.

![The backs of the same wraps, showing the official's initials across the tape](/photos/completed-gauze-and-tape-fight-wrap.jpg)

That signature across the back of the hand is the official confirming the wrap
was applied legally and has not been touched since. A fight wrap is a skill, and
a bad one costs a fighter a round. If you are competing out of Ironhand, I wrap
your hands.

## Common handwrap mistakes

- **Wrapping the thumb loop in the wrong direction.** The single most common
  error. It undoes the whole wrap.
- **Starting at the knuckles.** Always anchor at the wrist first.
- **Wrapping too tight.** White fingertips, tingling, or a pulsing sensation
  means unwrap and start over. You want compression, not a tourniquet.
- **Skipping the X's between the fingers.** Those are what actually spread the
  load across the hand.
- **Never washing them.** Wraps hold sweat. Unwashed wraps are how gym staph
  starts.
- **Wrapping over a fresh injury without seeing a doctor.** A wrap will let you
  keep training on a fracture. That is not a good thing.
