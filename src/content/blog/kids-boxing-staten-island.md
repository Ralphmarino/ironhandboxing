Parents ask a fair question before signing a kid up for boxing: *is this going
to teach my child to fight?*

The honest answer is that it teaches the opposite far more often. Here's why.

## Discipline arrives before technique

The first things a young athlete learns at Ironhand have nothing to do with
punching. Show up on time. Listen when a coach is talking. Wrap your own hands.
Shake hands with the person you worked with. Put the equipment back.

Kids who are used to being told to sit still all day get something different
here: a room where the physical energy is the point, but only inside a
structure. That combination is unusually good at building self-control, and it
tends to show up at school and at home before it shows up in the gym.

## Coordination that transfers everywhere

Boxing is a full-body skill. Feet, hips, shoulders and hands all have to
sequence correctly, in balance, while something is moving in front of you.
Young athletes who train it pick up other sports noticeably faster — the
body awareness carries.

## Confidence, not aggression

The kids who talk the most about fighting are almost always the ones who've
never done it. A child who spends a few months learning how hard this is
actually gets *less* interested in proving anything. They know what they can
do, which removes the need to demonstrate it.

That quiet version of confidence is what parents tell us they notice. Their kid
stands differently. Handles a bad day better. Stops shrinking in rooms.

## What the class looks like

Our Kids & Teens class runs **Fridays at 5:00 PM**. It's pads, bags, footwork
and conditioning — technique and fitness, not contact. If a young athlete later
moves toward competing, that's a separate conversation and a parent is part of
it from the start.

We've had children start as young as seven. There's a family rate of $150 per
student for two or more kids, because more than a few families end up sending
siblings once the first one starts talking about it at dinner.
